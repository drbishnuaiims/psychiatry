Definition

What is a migraine trigger?

Definition
A factor that, on exposure or withdrawal, raises the probability of an attack, usually within 48 hours (IHS).
  • Not a necessary cause: it may not always induce an attack, and attacks occur without triggers.
  • Most data come from retrospective surveys with recall bias. Prospective studies are few and conflicting.
Key question at the bedside
Is this factor causing the attack, or is it an early symptom of an attack already starting?

Hormonal

Menstrual migraine: a real trigger

  • Days 1–3 of menses carry about 2× risk of an attack (prospective data).
  • Menstrual attacks tend to be more severe, longer and more treatment-resistant, usually without aura.
  • Ovulation is not a trigger.
  • Oral contraceptives can worsen migraine in some. Pregnancy often improves it, mainly in the 3rd trimester.
Mechanism
Oestrogen withdrawal in the late luteal phase, with prostaglandin release.
Decision point
Pure menstrual migraine: consider stable oestrogen or perimenstrual prophylaxis, e.g. naproxen.

Why it matters
Only these agents reproducibly trigger attacks in most patients. They map onto treatment targets: CGRP and PACAP signal via cAMP, NO via cGMP.

Diet and weather

Food and weather: weaker than believed

  • 10–50% of patients blame food. Commonly named: alcohol, cheese, chocolate, caffeine withdrawal, missed meals.
  • Diary study of 327 patients: very limited effect of diet; beer even lowered risk.
  • Provocation trials: tyramine no different from placebo; chocolate negative or a trend; red wine positive only in pre-selected sensitive patients.
  • Weather: prospective studies mostly negative, but a susceptible subgroup likely exists.
Pearl
Pre-attack food cravings can make a food look like a trigger when it is a premonitory symptom.

Stress and lifestyle

Stress, sleep and activity

  • Stress: number of stressors does not rise before attacks, but perceived stress and coping worsen.
  • Sleep: both lack and excess are reported. Likely both a trigger and part of the attack process.
  • Exercise: rarely provokes attacks under controlled conditions. Inactivity may be premonitory.
  • Smoking, sex: small effect. Sex during an attack improved headache in many who tried it.
Do not confuse
Primary exertional headache: pulsating pain brought on only by exertion.

Clinical reasoning

Trigger or premonitory symptom?

True trigger
  • Reproducible
  • Precedes symptoms
  • Proven: hormones, GTN, CGRP
Premonitory
  • Craving, yawning, tiredness
  • Reduced coping with stress
  • Avoiding activity
Why it's hard
Triggers cluster: a long jog brings heat, dehydration, low glucose, a chocolate bar and fatigue. The single culprit is rarely identifiable.
Tell patients
Some factors occur before an attack but may be early symptoms, not causes.

Management

Does trigger avoidance work?

Evidence is thin
A small study showed ~50% fewer attacks with avoidance, but patients also took early analgesic and antiemetic, a major confounder.
  • No convincing trial shows avoidance works as prophylaxis, despite being standard advice.
  • Lab provocation with a patient's own reported triggers caused aura in only 11%.
  • Graded exposure (CBT-style) has been suggested but is untested.
Practical stance
Limit clear, reproducible triggers. Avoid long restriction lists, which add stress and may do nothing.

Take-home

Key points

  • Patient-reported triggers are common but poorly supported by prospective data.
  • Proven: oestrogen withdrawal and experimental agents (NO, CGRP, PACAP, prostaglandins).
  • Many 'triggers' are premonitory symptoms: cravings, tiredness, reduced stress tolerance.
  • Avoidance has no convincing trial support; use it selectively.
  • Always explain the gap between perception and evidence.
One line
Treat the attack and the biology, not a list of foods.
