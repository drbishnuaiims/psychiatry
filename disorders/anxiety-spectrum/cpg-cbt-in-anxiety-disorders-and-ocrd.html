🧩 CBT in Anxiety Disorders & OC-Related Disorders

Bedside / grand-round reference · Source: Reddy, Sudhir, Manjula, Arumugham, Narayanaswamy. IPS CPG, Indian J Psychiatry 2020;62(Suppl 2):S230–S250 · Aid only; individualise every patient.

Overview & Grading

Core messageCBT is first-line for most anxiety disorders and OCRDs, and helps those with inadequate drug response. Each disorder has its own CBT model even though exposure and cognitive restructuring recur. Specific phobia and BFRBs rely mainly on behavioural techniques (graded exposure; habit reversal).

Panic Disorder (PD)

Chronic, waxing–waning; recurrent unexpected panic attacks + anticipatory anxiety / worry about consequences + maladaptive behaviours (avoidance, safety behaviours, routine change). Agoraphobia often follows. Comorbid anxiety, mood, substance use.

Assessment

  • Patients fixate on physical symptoms → rule out medical causes. Assess severity and comorbidity (e.g. severe depression can interfere with PD therapy).

Behavioural analysis (Table 3): nature / frequency / duration; internal (chest tightness, breathlessness, nausea, palpitation, dizziness, unreality) and external triggers (alone, crowds, lifts, malls, bridges, theatres, public transport); feared consequences (death, heart attack, “going crazy”, losing control); maintaining factors (avoidance, safety behaviours, family accommodation); personal / family history; sociocultural factors, expectations; past attempts to cope.

Cognitive modelTrigger (internal / external) → catastrophic misinterpretation (“heart attack?”) → severe anxiety / panic → physical symptoms → perceived loss of control → avoidance and safety behaviours (going out alone, resting) → persistence of panic and agoraphobia.

Setting, efficacy, delivery

  • Mostly outpatient. In-patient if severe depression, other anxiety disorder, substance dependence, failed OPD therapy, severity precluding OPD.
  • CBT = first-line psychological treatment; antidepressants and CBT equally efficacious. Relapse 23% over 2–14 years.
  • Component NMA (72 studies): cognitive restructuring, interoceptive exposure, face-to-face = better efficacy / acceptability; muscle relaxation, breathing retraining, in vivo exposure, VR exposure = lower efficacy.
  • 10–15 weekly sessions; brief 6–7 sessions also effective. Minimal-contact (bibliotherapy, ICBT, self-exposure) beats waitlist; unsupervised self-help less effective than therapist-led.
  • Stop when target symptoms resolve (~10–15 weeks); joint decision to space sessions; prepare for termination early; supplemental CBT at medication withdrawal ↓ relapse. Psychological therapy effects last longer than drugs.
  • Maintenance: monthly CBT × 9 months kept gains to 21 months; relapse 5.2% vs 18.4%. Options: boosters only at relapse; mindfulness / ACT / supportive augmentation (need study in refractory cases).

Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

Excessive, hard-to-control worry about everyday things; restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension, sleep problems. Worry covers things within and beyond control (illness, future, community); coping = reassurance seeking, avoidance, mental preparation. Chronic with exacerbations at stress; <⅓ seek help (mostly primary care, for physical symptoms or depression); high comorbidity, ↑ medical illness.

Assessment

  • Clarify content and nature of worry; diagnose comorbid anxiety / mood; exclude endocrine, cardiopulmonary, neurological mimics; motivation, goals, expectations.
  • Scales (Table 6): Penn State Worry Questionnaire ultra-brief (3 items) · GAD-7 (7-item screen) · GAD Severity Scale (6 items).
  • Behavioural analysis (Table 7): worry content and somatic symptoms; deficits (problem-solving, communication, assertiveness); triggers (ill health, stress, work demands, change); feared consequences (losing control of thoughts, mental / physical illness); maintaining factors (avoidance, reassurance seeking, safety behaviours, need for control, intolerance of uncertainty, family accommodation); history, life events, worry-prone traits; sociocultural / modelling; past coping.

Models and formulation

Avoidance model of worry · intolerance of uncertainty · metacognitive · emotion dysregulation · acceptance-based. Common concepts: cognitive / experiential avoidance, positive and negative beliefs about worry, poor emotion understanding, poor problem orientation and problem-solving.

Intolerance-of-uncertainty model (Fig 3)Trigger → “what if?” thinking → intolerance of uncertainty + positive beliefs about worry (“worry prepares me”) + negative problem orientation → worry → anxiety, low mood → cognitive avoidance (suppression, distraction) → worry persists.

Setting, efficacy, duration

  • Outpatient; in-patient if severe comorbid anxiety, OCD, substance use, personality disorder, depression, self-harm.
  • CBT > waitlist; superiority over other therapies not established; helps comorbid depression (gains kept at 6 and 12 months); may beat applied relaxation. Individual = group, but individual has larger effect, earlier improvement, better adherence. ≤8 sessions effective; more for worry + depression. Mindfulness ≈ CBT. Internet CBT > waitlist / placebo, comparable to face-to-face and group. Transdiagnostic therapy useful with comorbidity.
  • Other variants (RCT-tested): applied relaxation, cognitive therapy, mindfulness / acceptance, metacognitive therapy, IU therapy, cognitive bias modification, emotion regulation, yoga-enhanced CBT, exercise, short-term psychodynamic.
  • Initial 12–15 weekly sessions → monthly; may be spread over a year. Stop when targets met or response plateaus. No agreed maintenance guideline; follow-up up to 1 year; open appointments for stress.

Specific Phobia

Excessive irrational fear of an object / situation, beyond voluntary control; avoided or endured with distress; impairment. Types: animals, natural environment (storms, heights), situational (lifts, planes), blood-injection-injury. ~75% have >1 phobia. Most prevalent anxiety disorder (8.3–13.8%), F:M ≈ 2:1; treatment-seeking delayed or low.

Assessment

  • Interview + functional analysis of cues, avoidance, safety behaviours, accommodation, arousal symptoms, automatic thoughts, impairment; separately for each phobia; early learning experiences.
  • Build a fear hierarchy with SUDS. Fear Questionnaire (Marks & Mathews): fear + avoidance (blood, travelling alone, social, dentist). Specific scales (spider, dental, blood-injury).

Models

Two-factor: classical conditioning acquires fear; operant (avoidance) maintains it. Social learning: modelling, informational. Cognitive: threat appraisal. → Treat by exposure, habituation / extinction, reappraisal.

Treatment

TechniqueDetails
Exposure (treatment of choice)Prolonged deliberate contact with feared stimulus without distraction / avoidance / safety behaviours until fear drops. Mechanisms: habituation + extinction. In vivo (most effective) > imaginal > in vitro / VR
Virtual reality exposureImmersive, interactive; efficacy in flying, heights, open spaces; large–moderate effect; gains maintained
ModellingVicarious learning – therapist or video (e.g. successful procedure) to promote approach
Systematic desensitisationReciprocal inhibition: deep muscle relaxation → fear hierarchy → pair cues with relaxation
Cognitive therapyAddresses threat appraisal, safety behaviours, avoidance; better than waitlist; useful when cognitions / safety behaviours dominate

PitfallsHigh attrition and refusal due to anxiety intolerance. Brief, inadequate exposure or subtle safety behaviours enhance fear (“incubation”).

Blood-injury phobiaVasovagal response: BP drops → fainting (not anxiety); avoids hospitals, needles. Use applied tension (raises BP) during exposure to specific cues. 5 sessions: rationale (diphasic BP: rise then drop) + note early cues → learn tensing arm, chest, leg muscles until warmth in face → next 3 sessions graded exposure (pictures of blood / injury → blood donation, minor surgery) while applying tension.
  • Mostly outpatient; functional in most roles. In vivo > imaginal / in vitro; exposure beats applied relaxation without exposure, cognitive restructuring alone, applied tension. Multi-session better than single-session; phobia type is not a moderator.

Social Anxiety Disorder (SAD)

Marked persistent fear of social (meeting, gatherings) or performance (public speaking) situations – fear of embarrassment, scrutiny, negative evaluation; avoided or endured. Arousal fears (blushing, sweating, palpitations), cognitions (mind going blank, losing control). More than shyness. Onset early-to-mid adolescence; F > M. Comorbid: MDD, other anxiety, substance, personality disorders (esp. avoidant PD).

Functional analysis (Table 12): antecedents (specific recent incidents); cognitions (NATs, dysfunctional assumptions, mind-reading, selective abstraction, fear of going blank / making a fool, others noticing anxiety); maintaining factors (safety behaviours e.g. gripping spoon, covering face, over-rehearsal; avoidance e.g. no eye contact, refusing promotions; perfectionism); early learning (parenting, anxious temperament, bullying, skill deficits); sociocultural.

Clark & Wells model (Fig 5)Enter social situation (seen as threat) → dysfunctional assumptions activated (“if they see I’m anxious they’ll think I’m a fool”) → self-focused attention, viewing self as others do → safety behaviours, avoidance, attention bias → somatic / cognitive symptoms become “evidence” of danger → more anxiety.

CBT (14–16 weekly sessions, 3–4 months; workbooks; systematic repeated practice is key)

  • Exposure challenges: unpredictable / variable social situations; brief by nature; self-focus reduces effective exposure; cognitive aspects → solve with in-session exposure, experiments, role-plays.
  • Applied relaxation: PMR → rapid relaxation, used at first sign of anxiety; coping tool – must not become a safety behaviour.
  • SST / assertiveness: most have performance deficits due to anxiety, not true skill deficit; modelling, role-play, rehearsal, feedback, homework. SST alone: little evidence; with exposure larger effect than exposure alone.
  • Setting: OPD; in-patient only for severe comorbidity (e.g. depression).

Efficacy and formats

  • Individual CBT largest effect; exposure ± cognitive restructuring strongest components. Individual ≈ group (Rapee-Heimberg CBGT). Brief CBT promising, little evidence.
  • Internet CBT (21 studies, guided + unguided): big reductions, gains 3 months–5 years; ≈ face-to-face; screen patients; controls mostly waitlist.
  • VR: generalisation to real life is hard without in vivo + homework; VRET alone < in vivo; ICBT and VRET promising.
  • Third wave / others: ACT, mindfulness, psychodynamic, IPT limited but emerging (similar components). MCT (detached mindfulness, attention training) – limited evidence.
  • Stopping: when anxiety measures (self-report + clinician) and avoidance improve; complete extinction unlikely in generalised SAD / avoidant PD – agree realistic goals. Continue exposure; boosters; gains maintained up to 1 year.

Obsessive–Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

Obsessions: repetitive intrusive unwanted thoughts / images / urges with distress. Compulsions: repetitive behaviours / mental acts to reduce distress. Insight: good → poor → delusional. Lifetime prevalence 1–3%; untreated = chronic waxing–waning. Comorbid: mood, anxiety, personality; childhood tic disorders, ADHD; BDD and hoarding often missed. SRIs and/or CBT are first-line.

Assessment

  • Identify comorbidity (mood, anxiety, personality); severity, nature of obsessions / compulsions, avoidance, family accommodation; underlying constructs: obsessive beliefs, sensory phenomena, disgust sensitivity.
Instrument (Table 15)Details
Y-BOCSSymptom checklist (current / past / principal – targets); severity scale = gold standard, consensus cut-offs for severity and response
OCI-R · MOCI · FOCI · Leyton · DY-BOCS · DOCS18-item self-report · 30-item true/false · screen + severity · 69-item · symptom dimensions · 20-item, 4 dimensions
InsightY-BOCS item 11 (0–4) · BABS (7 items, conviction / insight) · OVIS (11 items, overvalued ideas)
Related constructsFAS (13-item family accommodation) · OBQ (44-item beliefs) · MCQ-30 · DPSS (16-item disgust) · USP Sensory Phenomena Scale

Behavioural analysis (Table 16): triggers external + internal (hierarchy with SUDS 0–100); feared consequences; rituals – overt, mental, by proxy, sequence and function; avoidance / safety behaviours; family accommodation (taper gradually); insight and motivation (address before exposure); personality traits (distress tolerance, harm avoidance, perfectionism); sociocultural beliefs (e.g. cleanliness).

Models

  • Two-stage behavioural: classical conditioning acquires fear; compulsions / avoidance maintain via operant conditioning.
  • Cognitive: normal intrusions misinterpreted due to obsessive beliefs – threat overestimation, inflated responsibility, perfection / certainty need, thought–action fusion, over-importance and control of thoughts (from early experience, personality, critical events).
  • Cornerstone: ERP – graded exposure to triggers while preventing rituals / avoidance until anxiety falls; principle = habituation + disconfirmation; builds self-efficacy; recent emphasis on inhibitory learning.
CBT model (Fig 7)Trigger (touched a pen) → intrusive thought (“may be dirty”) → obsessive beliefs → misinterpretation (“I may get infected”) → anxiety → compulsion (washing) → relief (reinforcement) → no chance for habituation or disconfirmation. Avoidance, safety behaviours, suppression also reinforce.

Setting and delivery

  • Usually outpatient individual therapy: 2–3 months, 2–3 sessions/week, ~15–20 sessions. Barriers in India: trained therapist access, time. ICBT / computer CBT reduce contact (needs periodic therapist contact to prevent dropout); promising but limited controlled trials, not tested in India.
  • Intensive residential treatment (IRT): for severe / treatment-resistant OCD; medication + intensive ERP + milieu ± group; multidisciplinary supervision. 50–70% improve (uncontrolled); meta-analysis of 19 studies (n=2306): mean Y-BOCS ↓10.7, effect size 1.87; benefit to 6 months post-discharge.
  • Bergen 4-day concentrated exposure: 3–6 patients with equal therapists, “individual therapy in group setting”; very effective in uncontrolled studies.
  • Family interventions (reduce accommodation) help – involve family especially if high accommodation. Individual ≈ group (group less studied).
  • Inference-based therapy: doubts arise from internal narrative remote from sensory reality; therapy = reality reorientation; efficacy ≈ CBT in 2 trials; useful in poor insight (need larger trials).
  • ACT, MBCT untested in controlled trials; stress management, relaxation, psychodynamic – scant standalone evidence; D-cycloserine augmentation unestablished.
When to use whatCBT/ERP alone for mild–moderate OCD if therapist available; severe → CBT + SSRI; CBT = first augmentation for partial / non-responders to SSRIs.

Termination and follow-up

  • Judge progress against agreed goals; full remission predicts long-term outcome but may be unrealistic – terminate when most targets met, with boosters. Prepare in advance (continued exposure, homework, stress management, relapse prevention).
  • Post-CBT relapse ≥20%; better prognosis: lower initial severity, remission at end, no comorbidity, intensive CBT, homework compliance. Drugs may need to continue long-term, esp. when CBT was augmentation. Relapse-prevention programmes (psychoeducation, self-exposure, stress management, lifestyle); tapering phone / direct boosters.

Body Dysmorphic Disorder & Body-Focused Repetitive Behaviours

Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD)

  • Persistent preoccupation with perceived appearance defects + repetitive behaviours (mirror-gazing, grooming, reassurance-seeking, mental acts). DSM-5: insight specifier (poor insight ≠ delusional disorder) and muscle dysmorphia specifier. Comorbid MDD, SAD, substance use; high suicide risk. Prevalence ~2% (adults and adolescents), F > M.
  • Assessment: BDD-YBOCS – gold standard, 12-item semi-structured clinician scale, past week, sensitive to change. BDD Questionnaire – brief self-report screen.
  • Treatment: CBT = primary; SRIs as in OCD.
CBT model (Fig 9)Selective attention on minor flaws + overvalued appearance + comparison with “ideal” → negative appraisal, low mood → mirror checking / avoidance, camouflage (make-up), cosmetic surgery, social avoidance → temporary anxiety relief (negative reinforcement) → maladaptive beliefs persist.

Trichotillomania and skin-picking (excoriation) disorder

  • Both in OCRDs (DSM-5, ICD-11). Trichotillomania 1–3%, ~4× commoner in women; scalp most common, then eyebrows; triggers sensory, mood (anxiety, boredom, anger), cognitive; some “automatic” pulling; trichophagia in 5–20% → trichobezoar risk. Skin-picking 1–4% (limited data): urges to remove acne, scars, scabs; accessible sites (face, extensor arms, fingers). Anxiety, depression, OCD common.
  • Assessment: MGH Hair Pulling Scale (7-item self-report: urge, amount, control, distress, past 7 days); Keuthen Diagnostic Inventory for Skin Picking (DSM-5); screen comorbidity; functional analysis of antecedents and consequences.
  • Treatment: CBT first-line, mostly behavioural – habit reversal training (HRT), 4–20 sessions. DBT-augmented CBT (mindfulness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance) and ACT (accept urges without acting) each tested in single RCTs; similar strategies for skin-picking (HRT in few RCTs; ACT only uncontrolled).
