⚡ Psychiatric Disorders in Epilepsy

Bedside / grand-round reference · Source: Kedare & Baliga. IPS CPG, Indian J Psychiatry 2022;64(Suppl 2):S319–S329 · Aid only; individualise every patient.

Overview & Epidemiology

DefinitionsSeizure (ILAE/IBE 2005): transient signs / symptoms due to abnormal excessive or synchronous neuronal activity. Epilepsy (ILAE 2014): ≥2 unprovoked seizures >24 h apart, OR one unprovoked / reflex seizure with ≥60% recurrence risk over 10 years, OR an epilepsy syndrome.
70 millionpeople worldwide; 90% in developing countries
1.2–11.9 /1000adult prevalence, India
5.9–54.9%psychiatric disorders in epilepsy (up to 80% in TLE)
30%of epilepsy clinic patients had psychiatric admission; 10–20% on psychotropics
  • Comorbidity can pre-exist, cause or follow epilepsy, and occur at any time. Common: depression, anxiety, psychosis, personality change, cognitive problems, attention deficits.
  • Often missed – seizure control dominates care; clinicians unaware. Untreated → poorer treatment response, poorer QoL, early death (suicide, accidents).
  • More frequent than in asthma and healthy controls (two Indian studies). Epilepsy prevalence in psychiatric hospitals: 4.7% (UK), 9.7% (US VA).

Bidirectional hypothesis

Shared pathologyDepression, anxiety, psychosis may precede epilepsy. Depression: HPA hyperactivity, glutamate / GABA change, ↓ hippocampal and frontal volume. Psychosis: ↑ dopamine in mesial temporal / limbic regions with ↓ dopamine in ventro- and dorsolateral PFC.

Core Disorders

Depression

  • Pooled prevalence 22.9% (Scott 2017); Canadian survey (n=36,984) 17.4%; EPIC (US) 32.5%.
  • Risks: disability, stigma, poor support. PET: ↓ 5-HT1A binding in medial temporal regions (depression and TLE); shared frontal / temporal networks.
  • Worsens epilepsy course: poorer QoL, poorer treatment response, poorer surgical results.

Anxiety

  • Pooled 20.2% (Scott); EPIC 22.4%. Often with depression; missed because panic mistaken for ictal fear or seen as “natural reaction”.
  • Seen: panic, GAD, agoraphobia, social phobia, rarely OCD. Rule out thyroid / endocrine disease and drug side effects.

Psychosis

  • 7–12% of epilepsy; 5–8× general-population risk; TLE 7% vs 5.6% other epilepsy.
  • Risks: severe epilepsy; family history of epilepsy / schizophrenia; TLE (esp. with febrile seizures); hippocampal sclerosis on MRI; autoantibodies (anti-NMDA, anti-GABA-B, anti-VGKC; ~10%).
  • Ictal, postictal, interictal forms. Forced normalisation = psychosis emerging when seizures seem controlled. Chronic schizophrenia-like psychosis also occurs.

Pharmacotherapy Principles

EvidenceFew systematic studies; guidelines rely on clinical experience and open-label data, and mostly follow non-epilepsy principles – with extra attention to interactions and seizure threshold.

Pharmacokinetic interactions

  • Inducers (CBZ, phenytoin, phenobarbitone) ↑ CYP450 + UGT. Valproate is a broad inhibitor of CYP / UGT. Oxcarbazepine and topiramate: weak inducers (high dose). Newer AEDs: fewer interactions.
  • Antidepressants: SSRIs, mirtazapine, venlafaxine levels ↓ ~25%; bupropion up to 90%. TCA–AED interactions usually not clinically relevant. VPA slightly ↑ O-desmethylvenlafaxine; no other antidepressant interaction.
  • Antipsychotics: CBZ ↓ levels of all typical antipsychotics and aripiprazole, clozapine, olanzapine, paliperidone, quetiapine (may become undetectable), risperidone, ziprasidone. VPA–antipsychotic: generally not relevant.

Pharmacodynamic interactions

  • Positive: AED + SGA combinations used in mania. Negative: sedation, weight gain, haematological effects.

Pharmacological Management

Depression (goal: complete remission)

Depression in epilepsy
On depressogenic AED, or recently stopped a mood-stabilising AED? → discontinue depressogenic AED; switch to / restart mood-stabilising AED (CBZ, VPA, lamotrigine, gabapentin, topiramate)
Preferred: sertraline, citalopram (SSRIs) · mirtazapine · venlafaxine
Avoid / caution: fluoxetine, fluvoxamine (interactions), high-dose TCAs, immediate-release bupropion, maprotiline

Watch pharmacodynamic effects: hyponatraemia, sedation, weight gain, sexual dysfunction. Follow general guidelines for duration.

Mania / bipolar depression

Drugs of choice: CBZ, VPA, lamotrigine, gabapentin, topiramate. Lithium only second choice / augmentation – induces EEG abnormalities; with CBZ → encephalopathy.

Anxiety / OCD (Fig 2, Table 10)

DisorderTreatment
PanicCBT alone, or CBT + SSRI (sertraline, citalopram)
GADPregabalin (first line if epilepsy + GAD – synergistic), paroxetine, venlafaxine, imipramine
Social anxietySSRI: sertraline, paroxetine, escitalopram
PTSDSSRI: sertraline, paroxetine

SSRIs are first line for panic, social anxiety and PTSD.

Psychosis (Fig 3)

  • No direct studies → follow non-epilepsy guidelines but lowest effective dose, slow titration, close monitoring. Risperidone, aripiprazole preferred; clozapine only after failure of others. Long-acting injectables not recommended (can’t stop quickly if seizures).
Interictal psychosis
  • Treat as primary psychosis (schizophrenia protocols)
  • Continue ≥1 year after first episode; 2–5 years after multiple episodes; indefinite if repeated relapses, suicide attempts, violence
Postictal psychosis
  • Mild: oral BZD
  • Moderate: oral BZD + antipsychotic (risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine)
  • Severe / violent history: IM haloperidol + promethazine
  • Watch for paradoxical BZD response
  • Usually improves within a week; taper sedatives within 1–3 months
  • Prevention: good seizure control; continuous antipsychotic not recommended

ECT

  • For severe depression with suicidality / treatment resistance, and mania.
  • Issues: high seizure threshold from AEDs (need stronger stimulus, monitor seizure duration); risk of prolonged or tardive seizures. ECT raises seizure threshold over time – possibly beneficial for epilepsy.

Disability evaluation (RPWD Act 2016)

  • “Chronic neurological conditions” lists multiple sclerosis and Parkinson’s; non-traumatic epilepsy is not specifically named but intractable epilepsy may be certified as chronic neurological condition.
  • No clear method to calculate disability severity – guidelines urgently needed (travel, work, stigma).
  • Epilepsy + psychiatric disorder → certify under multiple disabilities, together with a neurologist.

Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder (PNES)

DSM-5 FND with attacks / seizures; ICD-10 dissociative convulsions. Seizure-like, paroxysmal, time-limited episodes with motor / sensory / autonomic / cognitive features but no epileptiform activity on video-EEG. Psychological basis.

10–40%of long-term epilepsy cases have dissociative convulsions
10–15%of dissociative convulsions have true epilepsy
1–16 ydelay from onset to diagnosis (LaFrance)
  • Commoner in women 26–32 y; also children (not preschool), elderly. Missed diagnosis → needless AEDs, toxicity, repeated admissions, unaddressed psychological issues.
  • Refer to specialised epilepsy clinic if seizures not controlled with AEDs >1 year (NAEC).

Management

Emergency: aim for symptom-free state – suggestion; social isolation (techniques); psychoeducate caregivers, gather support
Confirm at OPD: history, episode nature, video-EEG (gold standard), psychological tests; rule out epilepsy / other neurological disease
Neurologist tapers AEDs · build rapport · link psychological stress / trauma to attacks
CBT (coping, distortions); psychodynamic interpersonal, group, family therapy; strengthen support; treat comorbid anxiety / depression
Mixed disorder: continue AEDs but cut very high doses, avoid polytherapy; psychiatry–neurology liaison to avoid needless investigations

Primary careEEG, video-EEG and psychological tests may be unavailable – a careful clinical history is key.

Quick Revision

Numbers
  • Depression 22.9%; anxiety 20.2%; psychosis 7–12% (5–8×)
  • Suicide 3–7%; risk 4–5×, TLE 25×
  • Clozapine seizures 1 / 2.7 / 4.4%
  • TCA >200 mg, bupropion IR >450 mg
  • Postictal psychosis lucid 12–72 h; depression up to 2 wk
  • Post-surgery mood change 6–12 wk (25%)
  • Subjective cognitive complaint 44%
Drug choices
  • Depression: sertraline, citalopram, mirtazapine, venlafaxine
  • Avoid: fluoxetine, fluvoxamine, high TCA, bupropion IR, maprotiline
  • Psychosis: risperidone, aripiprazole; clozapine last; no LAI
  • GAD: pregabalin
  • Mania: CBZ, VPA, lamotrigine; lithium 2nd
  • ADHD child: methylphenidate
Pitfalls
  • Levetiracetam, zonisamide → psychiatric effects
  • CBZ lowers antipsychotic / quetiapine levels
  • Lithium + CBZ → encephalopathy
  • Panic ≠ ictal fear
  • Restraint worsens peri-ictal aggression
  • Don’t treat PNES with ever-higher AEDs
Self-test
  • Psychosis 2 days after a cluster of seizures? → Postictal psychosis (lucid interval); BZD ± antipsychotic; improve seizure control.
  • New aggression after levetiracetam? → treatment-emergent PBSE; switch to low-risk AED.
  • Eyes closed, 5-min asynchronous movements, normal prolactin? → Dissociative convulsions; video-EEG.
  • Epilepsy + GAD? → pregabalin.
