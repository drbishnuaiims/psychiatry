🧠 Clinical Practice Guidelines: Management of Schizophrenia

Bedside / grand-round reference · Source: Grover, Chakrabarti, Kulhara, Avasthi. IPS CPG, Indian J Psychiatry 2017;59(Suppl 1):S19–S33 · Update of IPS 2005 guidelines · Adults; aid only, individualise every patient.

Overview & Scope

Core messageSchizophrenia prevalence in India: 2–3 per 1000. Care is hampered by resource constraints, poverty, low education, poor access. Guidelines update IPS 2005 by emphasising what is new; for adults (children and elderly differ); not setting-specific; must be tailored to the individual.

Assessment & Setting

  • Cornerstone: detailed history (all sources, esp. family) + physical exam + MSE. Diagnose by current criteria (more reliable, aids communication and evidence application).
  • Mimics to exclude: mood disorders, substance-induced psychoses, psychoses secondary to physical illness. Definitive diagnosis may need time; in doubtful cases consider a medication-free observation period – balanced against risk of delaying treatment / harm. Diagnosis is a continuous process.
  • Make with great caution and sensitivity (enormous psychosocial consequences).
Basic (Table 1)
  • Assess patient + caregivers
  • Physical exam: BP, weight, BMI, waist circumference
  • Areas: severity, dimensions (reality distortion, disorganisation, negative, depressive, cognitive), comorbid physical / psychiatric / substance, risk to self and others, functioning, socio-cultural milieu
  • Basic investigations: haemogram, blood sugar and lipids, LFT, RFT, ECG (focus QTc)
Optional
  • Standardised rating scales
  • Cognitive testing
  • Neuroimaging: first-episode psychosis, neurological signs, non-response, elderly
  • Urine / blood drug screen with consent (high suspicion for comorbid substance use)
Caregiver assessment
  • Knowledge and understanding of illness
  • Attitudes / beliefs on treatment
  • Impact on them; personal and social resources

Ongoing: response, side effects, adherence, immediate environment, disability, other health needs, access, relationship with team. Treatment plan: needs-based, practical, feasible, flexible; consult patient, caregivers, staff; re-evaluate regularly.

Treatment setting

Least restrictive setting that meets needs of patients and caregivers; most patients treated as outpatients; inpatient beds scarce; family caregiver should accompany admitted patient; if no beds, inform family and facilitate admission at nearest facility.

Indications for inpatient care (Table 2)Suicidal behaviour endangering life · severe agitation / violence endangering others · refusal to eat endangering life · severe malnutrition · unable to care for self (needs constant supervision) · catatonia · medical / psychiatric comorbidity making outpatient care unsafe or ineffective.

Route

  • Acute agitation: parenteral. Liquid or mouth-dissolving forms for non-compliant patients.
  • Depots not used in acute agitation, except zuclopenthixol acetate (half-life ~20 h). Aid adherence; also on patient / relative preference; give a test dose; keep within standard dose and interval to prevent relapse. Rule: one drug by one route (fewer interactions, simpler observation).

Adequate trial and response

Adequate trialHighest tolerable dose for 6–8 weeks – except clozapine: ≥3–6 months. Antipsychotics reliably remit positive symptoms (goal of acute phase); negative symptoms only mild–moderate improvement; response markers for cognition unclear. Monitor regularly.

Non-response (Fig 2)

Adequate trial (adequate dose, ≥6 weeks) → adequate response: continue same dose; monitor side effects
Non-response → re-evaluate diagnosis and compliance
Pseudo non-response (poor compliance): find causes, fix; if due to intolerable side effects → change antipsychotic (oral / depot)
True non-response: change antipsychotic
Failure of 2 adequate trials (≥1 SGA) → clozapine
Inadequate response to clozapine → combine with ECT or another antipsychotic; more intensive psychosocial intervention
  • Clozapine earlier if violent, suicide risk, not responding, or intolerable side effects with 2 classes.
  • Antipsychotic combinations: limited evidence. Initial agitation – SOS, shortest time; avoid prolonged combos except in clozapine non-responders.

Adjuncts, ECT & Psychosocial Interventions

Adjunctive medicines (rationale, shortest duration, review each visit)

  • Lithium / mood stabilisers: agitated, overactive patients, or affective symptoms poorly responding.
  • Benzodiazepines: agitation, sleep disturbance.
  • Antidepressants: post-psychotic depression; avoid in florid psychosis.
  • Anticholinergics: no prophylactic use; start when EPS appears; lowest dose, shortest time.

ECT

Used frequently in India. Evidence in acute phase controversial: augmentation and acceleration of response; benefit mainly first few weeks; some merit in poor responders. Not useful in chronic schizophrenia; long-term use only exceptionally. Needs information, support, informed consent, proper administration, response / side-effect monitoring.

Possible indications (Table 7)
Catatonia · affective symptoms · need for rapid control · suicidal behaviour endangering life · severe agitation / violence endangering others · refusal to eat · past good response · non-response to adequate trial · augmentation of partial response · clozapine-resistant · unable to tolerate antipsychotics

Psychoeducation (Table 8)

Patient + family: simple brief explanations (illness, treatment, side effects, duration); give relatives time; careful, clear language; no blame; goal incl. adherence; take feedback before each session.

Components
  • Knowledge about aetiology, treatment, prognosis; introduce diagnosis; symptom dimensions
  • Treatment options, efficacy, side effects, duration; importance of adherence
  • Course and long-term outcome
Also
  • Substance abuse, marriage, communication, problem-solving, disability benefits
  • Relapse and early warning signs
  • Day-to-day stress; improving insight
  • Expressed emotion, communication; coping with residual symptoms

Psychosocial interventions

  • Integral to management; Indian evidence strongest for family treatments, rehabilitation, community programmes, yoga, cognitive remediation. Needs-based approach.
  • Family interventions: predominantly psychoeducational (informal, structured, group, integrated); emphasis on continued contact and adherence with emotional + practical support; tailored to background and needs.
  • Lifestyle / diet: all patients – exercise, diet change, smoking abstinence (↓ metabolic and cardiovascular risk).
  • Rehabilitation: culturally adapted; simple inexpensive strategies; vocational facilities scarce.
  • Individual supportive therapy: empathic relationship, good listening, advice, engagement, adherence, healthy lifestyle, stress minimisation – most commonly practised and highly valued.
  • Other (promising): home-based care, caregiver support groups, community interventions, cognitive training / remediation, yoga.
Basic components of any intervention (Table 9)Assess psychosocial factors of patient and family · wide range works in India · choice by need and resources · promote alliance with patient + family, ongoing engagement, information, adherence and healthy lifestyle, emotional and practical support.

Phases of Illness & Treatment

Prodrome (weeks–years): psychosocial first
Acute phase: florid symptoms, risk; safety, antipsychotic, alliance
Continuation (stabilisation): ~6–12 months; consolidate remission
Maintenance (stable): follow-up every 2–3 months; prevent relapse, recovery

Prodromal stage

  • Cognition, emotion, perception, communication, motivation, sleep change – weeks to years before psychosis. Labels: basic symptoms, attenuated positive symptoms, brief limited intermittent psychotic symptoms, schizotypal features, genetic risk + functional decline, non-specific general symptoms.
  • Conversion 25–40%. Predictors: genetic risk with functional drop, unusual thought content, suspiciousness / paranoia, social impairment, longer duration, depression, ↓ attention, substance use.
  • Management: needs-based. Antipsychotics may delay conversion but evidence not convincing for all; antidepressants help a subgroup; omega-3 preliminary benefit. Psychosocial first (CBT, cognitive therapy, stress management). Weigh drugs against side effects and dopamine supersensitivity / rapid-onset psychosis on stopping.

Acute phase (Table 11)

Comprehensive assessment; goals – patients: reduce symptoms, improve function, safety, ↓ stress; caregivers: ↓ distress, coping help; both: alliance, engagement, tailored information. Then: setting; antipsychotic (drug, dose, route, duration, response, combos); adjuncts; ECT; basic psychosocial; planning further treatment.

Continuation phase (Table 12)

Goals: consolidate remission, ↓ symptoms, prevent early relapse, ↓ stress, support adaptation after discharge. Continue medication at same dose for 6–12 months; keep engagement and adherence; support; more elaborate psychosocial work; monitor response, side effects, adherence.

Maintenance phase (Table 13)

  • Stable symptoms; negative symptoms may predominate, functional deficits evident. Goals: maintain / improve function and quality of life, personal recovery, treat exacerbations, manage adverse effects. Follow-up q2–3 months, more in crisis; regular family feedback.
  • Dose: balance side effects vs relapse. Stable patients without positive symptoms – reduce gradually ~20% every 6 months to minimum effective dose. After withdrawal, monitor relapse signs ≥2 years; treat re-emergence at once.

Highlights

  • EPS: more with high-potency FGAs but also SGAs. Acute (days–weeks; dose-dependent): dystonia, pseudo-parkinsonism, akathisia. Chronic (months–years; not dose-dependent, persists after stopping): tardive dyskinesia, dystonia, akathisia.
  • NMS: psychiatric emergency; commoner with FGAs but reported with almost all SGAs.
  • Sedation: high with chlorpromazine, clozapine, quetiapine (antihistaminic, antiadrenergic, antidopaminergic).
  • Anticholinergic (dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, retention, thermoregulation, cognition, delirium, hallucinations): commonest with clozapine, chlorpromazine; dose-dependent.
  • Cardiovascular: QTc >500 ms → torsades → VF / sudden death. FGAs: thioridazine, pimozide, high-dose IV haloperidol; SGA: ziprasidone (not shown to cause sudden death). Hypotension (antiadrenergic): clozapine, risperidone, quetiapine, chlorpromazine – start low, titrate slowly.
  • Hyperprolactinaemia: all antipsychotics cause SD; higher with FGAs and risperidone; females more sensitive.

Quick Revision

Key numbers
  • Prevalence 2–3 / 1000
  • Adequate trial 6–8 wk; clozapine 3–6 months
  • Clozapine after 2 failed trials (≥1 SGA)
  • Continuation 6–12 months, same dose
  • Maintenance: first episode 1–2 y; multiple ≥5 y
  • Dose reduction ~20% / 6 months
  • Relapse watch ≥2 y after withdrawal
  • Prodrome conversion 25–40%
  • Weight gain >7% → switch
  • QTc >500 ms
  • Adherence: ~50% non-adherent, 20–60% drop out
Drug picks
  • Treatment resistance, suicidality, TD: clozapine
  • Clozapine-resistant: + ECT
  • Acute violence: haloperidol / lorazepam inj.
  • Catatonia: lorazepam → ECT
  • Negative symptoms: clozapine, amisulpride (no proven)
  • Post-psychotic depression: antidepressant
  • Metabolic risk: avoid olanzapine, clozapine
Pitfalls
  • Don’t label before excluding mimics
  • Evaluate non-compliance before switching
  • No prophylactic anticholinergics
  • No depot for acute agitation (except zuclopenthixol acetate)
  • Antidepressants in florid psychosis
  • Prolonged antipsychotic combinations
Self-test
  • 6 weeks on risperidone 6 mg, no response, doubtful adherence? → assess adherence first; if true non-response switch.
  • Two adequate trials failed (one SGA)? → clozapine.
  • Patient gains 9% weight on olanzapine? → switch; else metformin / topiramate + lifestyle.
  • Young male, IM haloperidol, jaw spasm? → acute dystonia: parenteral anticholinergic.
  • Stable first-episode patient at 1.5 years? → consider gradual taper (~20% / 6 months) with monitoring ≥2 years.
