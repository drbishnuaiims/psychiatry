🫀 Psychosexual Health & Sexual Medicine in CL Psychiatry

Bedside / grand-round reference · Source: Rao, Banerjee, Tandon, Sawant, Jha, Manohar, Rao. IPS CPG, Indian J Psychiatry 2022;64(Suppl 2):S429–S446 · Aid only; individualise every patient.

Overview & Role of the CL Psychiatrist

Core messageSexual dysfunction (SD) is biopsychosocial. Don’t label it “functional” – medical illness, drugs, distress and couple factors co-exist, and correcting the medical cause alone is often not enough.
  • Sexuality has 3 classic dimensions: desire, attachment, reproduction. Sexual medicine (Masters & Johnson) = evaluation and treatment of sexual disorders.
  • CLP = interface of general medicine / surgery / paediatrics and psychiatry (overlaps psychosomatic medicine, pain, health psychology, neuropsychiatry). Psychiatrist acts as “advising consultant”. Ideal = liaison model; in India mostly consultation-only, training heterogeneous, research limited.
  • DSM-5: an SD is not a psychiatric diagnosis if a medical condition (or several) is the presumed cause – but boundaries are blurred (e.g. adjustment to cancer → ED, worsened by chemotherapy).
  • Routinely blaming “functional cause” → misdiagnosis, under-diagnosis, poor quality of life.
  • This CPG covers medical-illness / drug-related SD; general SD management is in earlier CPGs.

Epidemiology & frequency

  • SD (US NHSLS): women 43% vs men 31%. Ageing + drugs + ≥1 comorbidity ↑ arousal (women) / erection (men) problems 1.5×.
  • Commonest offshoots of medical disease: ED, PME, dyspareunia, hypoactive desire. Least: delayed ejaculation and “frigidity”.
  • Diabetic men develop impotence 10–15 years earlier. Commonest pair: vascular ED (CAD, PVD, CCF) and dyspareunia from vulvar vestibulitis.

Evaluation and management principles

Sexual complaint
First steps: medical, sexual, psychosocial, cultural history + focused physical exam + tailored labs
Sex education · shared decision-making · explore couple’s sexual needs
Specific tests / specialist referral if needed
Treatment: medical, psychological, counselling, lifestyle, surgical, prosthesis, devices → outcome: function, satisfaction, QoL (ICSM-5)
  • Rule out SD better explained by non-sexual psychiatric disorder, substances, medical illness, or major relationship stress.
  • Many medical causes are treatable and reversible – evaluate fully.
  • History incl. practices, beliefs, myths, couple dynamics; corroboration from partner; genito-pelvic examination.
  • Baseline sexual function is essential to separate drug-induced from other SD.
  • Treat endocrine disease (hypothyroidism), hormone deficiency (low testosterone), arthritis / physical limits; stop offending drug.
  • Sexuality ≠ intercourse: intimacy, closeness, touch; no “optimum” level – patient / couple perception guides treatment.
  • Document biopsychosocial plan; discuss with other clinicians; longitudinal, multidisciplinary follow-up.

Management of drug-induced SD (13 steps)

  1. Address sexual function, expectations, partner; healthy lifestyle, exercise, adherence to illness treatment
  2. Information to dispel fears
  3. Prefer lower-risk drug; actively monitor sexual function
  4. Lowest effective dose
  5. Schedule sex around dosing
  6. Switch within class (e.g. β-blocker → nebivolol, cardioselective)
  7. Drug holidays
  8. Specific antidotes if available
  9. PDE-5 inhibitor (sildenafil) when indicated
  10. CBT, supportive, sex therapy
  11. Exercise daily and before sex
  12. Vacuum pumps, vibrators
  13. Antihypertensive SD: switch (CCB, ACE-i / captopril) or add PDE-5i – PDE-5i add little or no BP change/serious AE with usual antihypertensives; organic nitrate + PDE-5i must be avoided entirely
PrincipleDo a risk–benefit analysis; stop or switch to a better-tolerated drug wherever possible.

↓ vasorelaxation, ↑ leukocyte adhesion
Atherosclerosis / thrombosis
↓ NO availability → ED + cardiovascular disease
Artery size hypothesisPenile arteries (1–2 mm) cannot compensate for atherosclerosis like coronaries (3–4 mm) → ED is a marker of asymptomatic CAD and may precede CAD by 3–5 years.
  • Hypertension: ED prevalence ≈ double that of normotensives; SBP >140 mmHg doubles ED vs <140. Comorbidity rises with age, severity, duration, other CVD risks. Pelvic arterial insufficiency (iliac–pudendal–penile) is the main cause of ED in men >50.
  • Heart failure: LV dysfunction ↓ output, ↓ activity / exercise tolerance, hormonal imbalance, endothelial dysfunction, drugs → ↓ libido, ED, ↓ performance and satisfaction.
  • Post-MI: many develop SD, yet only 41% of patients and 31% of partners received sexual information by 1 year. Performance anxiety; vaginal dryness. Education is vital.

Psychological impact

  • Feel “less attractive / feminine”; negative sexual self-schema (breast, gynaecological) → poorer outcomes.
  • Low drive, fear / aversion; mood disturbance; antidepressants worsen function.
  • Fear that sex harms tissue or healing; misconceptions; poor pre-illness communication (fears mistaken for lack of interest).
  • HPV-related cancers: shame, guilt, stigma, fear of recurrence.
  • Plan: awareness among oncologists, timely assessment, treat SD with liaison.

Diabetes, Thyroid, HPG axis

Diabetes

  • MMAS: 3× risk of ED (type 1 and 2); ED prevalence 35–75%.
  • Male: ED, desire / arousal, orgasm / ejaculation problems; multifactorial: metabolic, vascular (micro/macro-angiopathy, endothelial), neuropathic (impaired sensory input, parasympathetic), hormonal (↓ T), psychological (depression, anxiety), local (phimosis, Peyronie’s) → ↓ NO.
  • Strict glycaemic control; reversal after control is not very successful.
  • Women: association less conclusive but SD more prevalent; more psychosocial.

Neuroendocrine axes

  • HPA, HPG, HPT, hypothalamic–neurohypophyseal – alteration in any → SD. HPG central. “Hypothalamic” amenorrhoea of stress / eating disorders; hypogonadism of Cushing’s.
  • Endocrinopathies in ED: low T 15% > hyperprolactinaemia 13.7% > hypothyroidism 3.1%. Androgen deficiency in 2–33% of ED. Diagnosis by blood levels.
  • Hypogonadism: primary (Klinefelter, cryptorchidism) or secondary (head trauma, prolactinoma, pituitary surgery, drug abuse). T replacement improves drive and PDE-5i response.

Thyroid hormone acts directly on ejaculation or via testosterone; both states alter sex hormones peripherally and centrally.

Ejaculation, prolactin, dopamine

  • Low T → delayed ejaculation; high T → PME.
  • Prolactin = surrogate marker of serotonin activity; high prolactin suppresses ejaculation. At orgasm dopamine peaks and prolactin is suppressed; afterwards prolactin spikes and dopamine falls → refractory period. Dopamine and prolactin inversely related.
  • Hyperprolactinaemia: 1–5% of ED men; ~50% microprolactinoma and 75% macroprolactinoma report ↓ desire / ED. Women: ↓ arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction. Chronic ↑ → hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.
  • GH: deficiency or excess → ↓ desire, arousability (both sexes), ED.

Hypersexual Disorder (HSD)

Not in DSM-5. Intense sexual urges / fantasies → distress or dysfunction: excessive sex, masturbation, pornography, computer-assisted sex. Seen as compulsion, addiction or impulse-control disorder. Also: erotomania, compulsive masturbation, sexual compulsivity. Neurobiology: thalamus, mammillary body, amygdala, PFC, cingulate, hippocampus, nucleus accumbens, caudate, VTA, raphe, substantia nigra.

Think of 3 possibilities(1) distinct disorder / problematic behaviour, (2) symptom of another disorder or medical condition, (3) normophilic high-end sexual activity. Cycle: urge → behaviour → satiation (sexual + post-sexual) → urge; incongruence and cognitive abeyance.

Proposed DSM-5 criteria (Reid)

≥6 months of recurrent, intense sexual fantasies, urges, behaviours with ≥4 of 5: (1) excessive time on fantasies / planning / acting; (2) in response to dysphoria or stress; (3) repeated failed efforts to control; (4) ignoring physical / emotional risk; (5) significant distress / impairment. Not due to substances or medical condition; age ≥18; specify type.

Subtypes (Kaplan & Krueger)

  • Excessive masturbation 50–75%; pornography 50–60% dependent
  • Consenting-adult sex: 7% regular sex workers, 12% unprotected multiple partners, 21% affairs
  • Cybersex / sexting; telephone sex (~37%); strip clubs (+ alcohol, guilt)

Treatment (Fig 8)

Medical evaluation: general, neurological, neuropsychiatric, psychological, substances, drugs
Assess impact: Hypersexual Behaviour Inventory, Sexual Compulsivity Scale, HD Screening Inventory
Biopsychosocial: Biological – SSRIs (fluoxetine 60–80, fluvoxamine 100–200 mg/day), TCAs (clomipramine 150–300), naltrexone 50–150, MPA 300–500 mg IM, cyproterone 100–300 mg/day (or 300–600 mg weekly IM), leuprolide 7.5 mg monthly IM, triptorelin 3.75 mg, lithium / mood stabilisers
Psychosocial – behaviour therapy, CBT, mindfulness, group, 12-step self-help, couple therapy

Gender Dysphoria & Gender-affirming Care

  • Replaced “gender identity disorder” in DSM-5: marked unease with biological sex vs gender identity; may impair function, cause anxiety / depression. Gender nonconformity alone is not a disorder – evaluate if distress. Seen in adolescents, children; includes disorders of sex development (gender assigned by parents / physicians).
  • Stigma, cultural issues, little medical teaching → depend on WPATH SOC v7 and Endocrine Society guidelines. Goals: resolve distress, affirm identity.
  • Team: psychiatrist, psychologist / counsellor, plastic surgeon, endocrinologist, urologist, gynaecologist (+ paediatrician, paediatric endocrinologist for children / adolescents).
Diagnosis (DSM-5 / ICD-11) + screening tools
Treat depression / anxiety; psychotherapy / counselling
Social transition – real-life experience, ≥1 year (reversible)
Cross-sex hormones ≥12 months + ongoing psychotherapy
Gender-affirming surgery (WPATH readiness + eligibility)

Role of the mental health professional (Table 15)

  • Only a qualified MHP diagnoses; trained in transgender care (other trained medical professionals if no MHP). Psychological evaluation + psychosocial functioning; discuss options, comorbidity; psychoeducate on identity / expression, comfort, social support.
  • Document transition (timeline, coping, commitment); family education; refer for hormones if criteria met; liaise with endocrinologist about expectations. Keep hormone record (needed for some surgeries).
  • Surgery letters: 2 referral letters (persistent dysphoria, capacity, consent, legal age, comorbidity controlled); 1 letter for breast / chest surgery; none for facial feminisation / masculinisation or thyroid laryngoplasty. Individualise; discuss goals, expectations, cost, aesthetics, recovery, complications.
  • Educate about legal document changes; provide peer-support referral.
  • India: Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act 2019 allows gender-affirming surgery and name change via set procedure. Need teams, curriculum reform, MHP awareness.

Psychosocial toolbox (Table 5)

  • Reassurance, sex education; reduce barriers (stigma, embarrassment, misinformation)
  • Lifestyle: exercise, yoga, vascular risk control, nutrition, weight, tobacco / alcohol
  • Treat fear of recurrence after AMI / CVA; encourage self-stimulation for singles; link sexuality with intimacy; positive self-talk
  • Dyspareunia: pelvic floor exercises, vaginal containment, positions, progressive relaxation · PME: sensate focus, stop-start, squeeze
  • Couple / sex therapy with homework; mindfulness group; CBT; coping / illness-perception work (cancer, genital surgery); tailored work for sexual minorities on HRT
